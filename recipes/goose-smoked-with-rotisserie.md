# Goose (Smoked with Rotisserie)

**Source:** Geoff's smoked goose, with techniques adapted from Hong Kong–style roast goose and Serious Eats (crisp-skinned roast goose)

**Prep Time:** 2 days (includes 36–48 hour dry brine)
**Cook Time:** 2–2½ hours, plus 20 minutes resting
**Yield:** 4 servings

A whole goose smoked on a rotisserie on a gas barbecue, flavoured with Geoff's Poultry Mix and apple, cherry or hickory smoke. Two borrowed techniques get the skin crisp:

- **Hong Kong–style roast goose:** a vinegar skin wash, here made with apple cider vinegar and maple syrup.
- **Serious Eats crisp-skinned goose:** a boiling-water blanch, a salt and baking powder dry brine, and a long, low render.

## Timeline

| When | Step |
|------|------|
| 2 days before | Trim, prick, blanch, season cavity, dry brine — fridge uncovered |
| Night before | Brush skin with maple-cider wash — back in fridge uncovered |
| Cook day | Truss, spit, smoke ~1 h, low render ~45–60 min, glaze and crisp 20–30 min, rest 20 min |

---

## Ingredients

### Goose

- 1 whole goose (about 3 kg), thawed; wing tips, wishbone, neck and giblets removed and reserved

### Cavity rub

- 2 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt (or 1¼ tsp Morton's)
- 1 tbsp Geoff's Poultry Mix (sage, thyme, rosemary)
- ½ tsp black pepper

### Dry brine (skin)

- 4 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt (or 2½ tsp Morton's)
- 1½ tsp baking powder
- 1 tbsp Geoff's Poultry Mix
- 1 tsp black pepper

### Maple-cider skin wash

- 2 tbsp maple syrup
- 2 tbsp apple cider vinegar
- 1 cup boiling water

### Maple glaze

- 2 tbsp maple syrup

### Cider goose jus (optional)

- Reserved neck, wing tips, wishbone and giblets
- 1 tbsp minced goose fat, plus 2 tbsp rendered goose fat
- 1 onion, sliced
- 3 sprigs thyme
- 4 sage leaves
- ½ cup dry hard cider
- 1 tbsp Calvados or brandy (optional)
- 2 cups low-sodium chicken stock
- 1 cup water
- 2 tbsp all-purpose flour
- Salt and black pepper, to taste

**Special Equipment:**

- Rotisserie spit and motor
- Large stockpot and clean rubber gloves, for blanching
- Smoker box and apple, cherry or hickory wood chips, soaked 30 minutes
- Drip pan
- Butcher's twine
- Wire rack and tray, for the fridge
- Probe thermometer
- Heavy cleaver

## Instructions

### Goose

1. **Trim the goose (2 days before):** Remove the giblets and neck. Cut off the wing tips at the joint — on a spit they burn before the bird is done. Remove the wishbone for easier carving (same method as a turkey). Save the neck, wing tips, wishbone and giblets for the jus. Trim the loose neck skin and pull out the pale, lumpy fat pads inside the cavity. Save them for rendering.

2. **Prick the skin:** With a trussing needle or the tip of a paring knife, prick the skin at **1 cm (½ in) intervals** all over, front and back, without piercing the meat. Concentrate on the fattiest areas — under the wings and around the thighs. This lets the fat escape on the spit.

3. **Blanch:** Bring a large stockpot two-thirds full of water to a boil. Wearing clean rubber gloves, hold the goose by the legs and lower it neck end first until half submerged, for **1 minute**. Lift it out, drain, then hold it by the wings and submerge the tail end for **1 minute**. The skin will tighten and start to render. Pat completely dry, inside and out.

   *If the bird won't fit in your pot:* set it on a rack in the sink and slowly pour about **3 L boiling water** over the skin, turning to cover all sides.

4. **Season the cavity:** Mix **2 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt** (or 1¼ tsp Morton's), **1 tbsp Geoff's Poultry Mix and ½ tsp black pepper**. Rub all over the inside of the cavity. Leave the cavity empty — it stays open on the spit.

5. **Dry brine the skin:** Combine **4 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt** (or 2½ tsp Morton's), **1½ tsp baking powder, 1 tbsp Geoff's Poultry Mix and 1 tsp black pepper**. Sprinkle evenly over all the skin, including under the wings and legs. Use just enough to coat — you may not need it all. Set the goose breast side up on a wire rack over a tray and refrigerate **uncovered for 36–48 hours**.

6. **Maple-cider skin wash (night before):** Wipe off any visible white residue from the dry brine. Whisk **2 tbsp maple syrup and 2 tbsp apple cider vinegar** into **1 cup boiling water**. Brush all over the skin in thin, even coats. Return to the fridge uncovered overnight until the skin is taut, dry and slightly translucent.

7. **Truss and spit (cook day):** Tie the legs together and the wings tight to the body with butcher's twine. Leave the cavity open so hot air and smoke can move through it. Run the spit through the centre, secure the forks, and **check the balance** by rolling the spit on your palms. Adjust until it doesn't flop to one side. Let the goose stand at room temperature 30–45 minutes while the barbecue heats.

8. **Set up the gas barbecue:** Remove the cooking grates. Place a drip pan with about **2.5 cm (1 in) warm water** directly under where the goose will turn. Make sure it covers both ends — fat runs out of the open cavity as well as off the skin. Fill a smoker box with **apple, cherry or hickory wood chips** (soaked 30 minutes) and set it over the far-side burner. Light that burner on high until the chips smoke, then turn it to medium. Leave the burners under the goose **off** (indirect heat). Use the rear rotisserie burner on medium if you have one; otherwise use the opposite end burner on low to medium. Close the lid and bring the barbecue to **250–275°F**.

9. **Smoke (first hour):** Mount the spit, start the motor and close the lid. Hold **250–275°F**, topping up the chips after 30–40 minutes. Smoke takes best on the bare, dry skin, so do all smoking in this phase.

10. **Low render (next 45–60 minutes):** Let the smoker box burn out and hold the barbecue at **275–300°F**. Low, steady heat renders out the fat under the skin without drying the meat. At the changeover, carefully pull the drip pan. A 3 kg goose renders 1½–2 cups of fat. Pour the fat and water into a large bowl to separate, then return the pan with fresh warm water to prevent flare-ups. Insert a probe thermometer into the thickest part of the thigh, away from the bone and spit.

11. **Glaze and crisp (last 20–30 minutes):** When the thigh reads about **165°F**, brush **2 tbsp maple syrup** onto the turning goose 2–3 times, 10 minutes apart. Turn the rear burner (or end burner) to high and raise the barbecue to about **375–425°F** until the skin is deep mahogany and crisp. Watch closely — maple burns fast. The goose is done when the thigh reads **at least 175°F** and the breast **165°F**. Total cook time is roughly 2–2½ hours for a 3 kg bird; go by temperature, not the clock.

12. **Rest, chop and serve:** Lift the spit off, remove the forks and twine, and rest the goose for **20 minutes**. Chop through the bone into bite-sized pieces with a heavy cleaver. Serve with the cider goose jus.

### Cider goose jus (optional)

Make this while the goose dry-brines; it keeps 5 days in the fridge.

1. **Brown the trimmings:** Cut the neck and wing tips into 7 cm (3 in) pieces. In a saucepan, render **1 tbsp minced goose fat** over medium heat. Add the neck, wing tips, wishbone, heart and gizzard (keep the liver aside), and brown well, 8–10 minutes.

2. **Add the aromatics:** Add **1 sliced onion, 3 sprigs thyme and 4 sage leaves**. Cook until the onion browns, about 10 minutes.

3. **Deglaze and simmer:** Deglaze with **½ cup dry hard cider** and **1 tbsp Calvados or brandy**, if using. Add **2 cups low-sodium chicken stock and 1 cup water**. Simmer, partly covered, for 2 hours, topping up with water to keep the bones mostly submerged.

4. **Strain:** Strain, pressing on the solids, and skim off the fat. You should have about 2 cups of stock.

5. **Thicken:** To finish, cook **2 tbsp rendered goose fat** with **2 tbsp flour** until light brown, about 5 minutes. Gradually whisk in the hot stock. Simmer until slightly thickened, 3–6 minutes.

6. **Finish:** Add the finely chopped liver if you like and simmer 1 minute more. Season with salt and black pepper.

## Notes

- **Why keep it whole:** a whole goose cooks more evenly and stays moister than a spatchcocked or broken-down bird.
- **Why a dry brine, not a wet one:** a wet brine adds moisture to the skin, which is the opposite of what the blanch, dry brine and skin wash are working towards.
- **Why the cavity stays open:** with only a dry rub inside, there is no marinade to drain onto the burners, and the open cavity lets heat and smoke reach the inside of the bird.
- **Why baking powder:** it raises the skin's pH, which helps it brown and blister crisp (Serious Eats technique).
- **Why blanch and render low:** blanching tightens the skin and starts the render. The long stretch at 275–300°F melts out the thick fat layer before the high-heat finish crisps the skin.
- **Why glaze only at the end:** maple syrup brushed on early will char on a gas grill over 2 hours.
- **Wood:** apple and cherry give a mild, sweet smoke. Hickory is stronger — use it alone for a bolder flavour, or mix it half and half with apple.
- **Rendered fat:** strain the drip-pan fat and the trimmed cavity fat (rendered slowly in a pan) and keep it for roast potatoes.
